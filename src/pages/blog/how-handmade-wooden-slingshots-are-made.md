The outline of a wooden slingshot can be drawn in a few minutes. Making that outline feel settled in the hand takes much longer.

Most of the work is not dramatic. It is looking at grain under good light, removing a little material, turning the frame over, and checking it again. A finished piece may appear simple because the transitions are clean. Getting those transitions right is where the hours go.

This is the general sequence I use in the workshop. Individual woods and frame shapes change the details, but the order remains much the same.

## It begins with the actual piece of wood

Species matters, but the individual blank matters more. Before drawing a frame, I check both faces and all four edges for checks, knots, short grain, and abrupt changes in direction. The fork shoulders need continuous grain running through an area that will become relatively narrow.

I also consider weight. A very dense blank can suit a compact frame, while a lighter wood leaves room for a fuller handle. The design should respond to the material rather than forcing every blank into one standard pattern.

Our guide to [choosing wood for a handmade slingshot](/blog/choosing-wood-for-handmade-slingshot/) explains why a beautiful species name is not a substitute for sound grain.

## The template is only a starting point

A paper outline establishes fork width, overall height, and the broad proportions of the handle. It does not decide the final grip. Once the profile is cut, the frame is still flat and surprisingly anonymous.

I leave extra material around the shoulders and handle at this stage. It is easy to remove wood later and impossible to put it back. On an asymmetric frame, I also mark the intended hand and grip orientation before shaping begins. Those pencil lines prevent an attractive curve from drifting into the wrong place.

## Shaping happens from several directions

The front view gets most of the attention in photographs, but comfort is created in three dimensions. The side profile determines the palm swell. The back edge affects finger clearance. The transition beneath the forks decides whether a pinch or thumb-supported hold feels natural.

I work gradually and pick the frame up often. A hard ridge can be invisible in a front photograph yet obvious to the hand. The goal is not to make every surface equally round. Some areas need a clear reference edge, while others should disappear into the palm.

This is also why two handmade frames with the same 80 mm outer width can feel different. Dimensions establish scale; shaping establishes character.

## Fork tips need careful preparation

Band attachment areas must be smooth and predictable. Sharp corners can damage latex, and uneven surfaces can make an attachment less secure. Traditional wrap-and-tuck tips and screw-down clamps require different preparation, but neither should rely on roughness or excessive force.

The edges are eased, the surfaces are checked, and any hardware is fitted before final finishing. Installing hardware late risks marking a surface that has already taken hours to prepare.

## Sanding is inspection, not just polishing

Sanding removes tool marks, but it also reveals the quality of the shaping. Under raking light, a shallow hollow or a flat spot becomes easy to see. I move through the grits without rushing and clean the surface between stages so loose abrasive does not create new scratches.

The frame is handled throughout this process. A surface can look flawless and still have one uncomfortable edge. If my fingers keep returning to the same spot, that area needs more work.

## Finish should still feel like wood

I prefer a cured oil-and-wax style finish for many solid-wood frames. It brings out the grain, offers sensible protection from handling, and keeps the surface tactile. A thick glossy coating may look impressive under studio lights, but it can make a working handle feel less direct.

Finishing also requires patience. Each coat needs time, and the frame should not be assembled or photographed while the surface is still soft. The final sheen comes from preparation and curing, not from leaving excess finish on the wood.

## Final inspection happens before photography

I inspect the fork area, attachment surfaces, hardware, and full frame under bright light. Then I hold it as intended and check for movement, roughness, or any new line in the grain. Measurements are taken from the finished piece rather than copied from the original drawing.

Only then is the slingshot photographed. Because hand shaping produces small differences even within the same pattern, Zenith Fork Studio lists one-of-one frames as the exact piece pictured. The photographs are part of the specification, not decoration.

A handmade wooden slingshot is finished when the material, measurements, and grip agree with one another. The outline may start the process, but the repeated checks at the bench are what make the object feel resolved.

Inspect any frame and bands before use, wear eye protection, use a secure backstop, and comply with local law.
