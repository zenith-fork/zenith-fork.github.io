The Flying Tiger round-tip and Large Mantis solid-wood slingshots both have an 80 mm outer fork width. The Flying Tiger is 120 mm high with 14 mm tips and a narrow sculpted waist. The Large Mantis is 130 mm high with 17 mm tips, broader shoulders, and a fuller palm swell. Choose between them by handle shape and fit, rather than assuming the larger frame performs better.

Seen separately, both frames look like compact wooden slingshots. Put their measurements beside each other and the difference becomes easier to describe. The Mantis carries more wood through the upper handle; the Flying Tiger draws the eye toward its waist and deep U-shaped opening.

This comparison concerns the two particular solid-wood listings linked below. The Flying Tiger pattern name also appears on our Desert Ironwood quick-clamp model, which is a separate product with different attachment details.

## Flying Tiger: a more defined waist

The [Flying Tiger round-tip solid-wood frame](/products/fork-flying-tiger-solid-wood-round-tip-80/) has a narrow section beneath the forks before the handle widens toward its base. That change in outline gives the fingers a clear reference point.

It is the first one I would compare if you already enjoy a frame with a slender waist. Its shorter height may also appeal if you dislike extra handle extending below the palm. Neither observation amounts to a universal small-hand recommendation: side thickness and the position of your fingers still matter.

## Large Mantis: more volume around the palm

The [Large Mantis solid-wood frame](/products/fork-large-mantis-solid-wood-80/) has broader shoulders and a rounded handle. Its extra 10 mm of height and fuller outline give it a different presence even though the outside fork width matches the Flying Tiger.

Consider it if thin handles leave your fingers searching for contact. Look especially at the side photographs: a palm swell is a three-dimensional shape, and a straight front view only shows part of it.

The fuller handle does not establish which hand it suits. With asymmetric shaping, the side on which material is added matters as much as the amount. Confirm handedness and the intended hold before ordering.

## What the wider tips do—and do not—tell you

The Mantis has 17 mm tips compared with the Flying Tiger's 14 mm tips. Tip width describes the available geometry at the attachment area. It does not by itself specify a suitable band width, attachment procedure, or draw weight.

The listings identify brass tip plates on both pieces. Ask for the intended attachment method rather than assuming every brass fitting is a screw-down quick clamp. A close-up is useful when comparing hardware that looks similar at gallery size.

For more on the measurements, see [what an 80 mm fork width actually means](/blog/what-does-80mm-fork-width-mean/).

## Is the Mantis worth the higher price?

The listed difference is USD 60. That buys a different piece and pattern; it is not a measured increase in accuracy, speed, or strength. No comparative performance test is being claimed here.

If the fuller handle and grain of the Mantis are what you want, the price may make sense to you. If the Flying Tiger's outline suits your hold better, paying more for a larger frame will not resolve that preference. With one-of-one woodwork, the particular object matters.

## A useful check before deciding

Measure a frame you already find comfortable and compare its height, waist, and side profile with these listings. If you do not have one, ask for a photograph with a ruler and the missing handle measurements. Include which hand will hold the frame and whether you use pinch grip or thumb support.
